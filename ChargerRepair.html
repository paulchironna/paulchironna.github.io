NAPA Battery Maintainer Repair

This 1.5 amp battery maintainer came into the shop I work at on an Edco walk-behind saw. The customer complained that it was no longer functioning and that they were going through a new battery every few months. I replaced it with a new one at their request and decided to give reviving the old one a try before sending it to it's fate at the landfill.

Visual Inspection

I used my hot air soldering iron and a scalpel to carefully separate the case and gain access to the circuit. With the board exposed, I began a visual inspection to hopefully identify any obvious signs of failure. I quickly found a couple strong clues.

Burnt up resistor coming off the positive rail
Lots of visible corrosion on the legs of the rectifier diodes

Implications

The corrosion on the positive half of this rectifier and crispy resistor seemed to imply to me that excessive heat was present in this area of the circuit for a longer period of time. From this point, I came up with a plan: isolate and test all the components that are fed directly off this rail, especially those that branch off into the circuit fed by the failed resistor. I wanted to look specifically for cracked solder joints and lifted pads.

Test

Schematic

I discovered the pad for the thru-hole resistor that had burnt up almost immediately. All the corrosion needed to be cleaned, lifted pads needed repair, and the rest of the circuit needed to be assessed as well. To start, I decided to trace and measure everything on the board to get a full picture of how the maintainer operates, and then I'd be able to assess what other components I should evaluate outside of the obvious. With a schematic made and the rest of the components spec'd out, I could also begin to try to figure out what value replacement was needed for the burnt up resistor.

To calculate the resistor value, I had to evaluate what its role is. Its placed directly after a diode in series with the Vcc to the op-amp and biases the Zener diode as well. Admittedly, I was lacking the knowledge of why you would choose which value series resistor for a Zener. After some research, I found that the specs of the Zener could be plugged into the equation R1=VS−VZ/IZT+IL. However, I could not identify the specific Zener used. To get a good approximation I had to think about what the goal of this circuit is: we're maintaining battery voltage at ~12VDC. I measured 16VDC coming into the rail. The op-amp should draw only a few mA and most 12v Zeners I found had a knee current spec of 0.25. Leaving some room for other components downstream, I decided a 100 ohm resistor would be best to use. I'd get roughly a 4 volt drop and enough current to properly bias my Zener so that it clamps properly, while also enough so as to not create a lot of heat and power loss.

Waveform captured right after the BNC input pin
Blown resistor on CH1 input path, likely due to overvoltage/current condition

Soldering

With the failed resistors identified, I carefully desoldered them, cleaned the area thoroughly, and replaced them with 82Ω 1% 1/10W SMD resistors. I then injected my test signal and verified that a good square wave was now present beyond the jack into the attenuator circuit.

New SMD resistor soldered onto CH2 input path
Test setup to verify repair

Function Restored

With the new resistors installed and connections verified, I powered the scope back on and ran test signals into all four channels. Each channel properly displayed a clean 1KHz square wave. Problem solved!

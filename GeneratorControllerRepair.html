Generator Controller Repair

A brand-new Wacker Neuson generator came into the shop with a no-crank no-start condition. It was found that there was no voltage present on the starter output pin during crank attempts. The DSE 73110 Mk II controller was replaced under warranty, but I was fortunate enough to be able to keep the old one to bring home and try to fix!

Verification

To begin, I started with the cardinal first step of the diagnostic process: verify the complaint. To do this, I just had to get my hands on a schematic to verify pinout, hook up 14-24VDC to the supply pins, and jumper out the E-Stop signal pin to B+ voltage. The case was easy to get apart, so I exposed the board as I set up for testing so I could simultaneously do a visual inspection of the circuit as well.

Controller Wiring Diagram
Power pins and E-Stop jumper installed

Testing Results

With power applied and the E-Stop bypassed, I attempted to activate the starter output by following the startup sequence on the controller. As expected, the complaint was verified - no voltage output present on the starter pin during crank attempts.

Video taken during crank attempt with 0 output

Diagnostic Strategy

Now that I had verified that there truly was no output present on pin 5, it was time to start following the output path upstream. I noted that I could hear the relay contacts clicking, so I was not very worried about anything on the control side. Being that this was a brand new controller, I began to heavily suspect that we had a cold solder joint either on the relay output pin itself or on the joint for the pin header. I couldn't yet rule out that possibly the power input to the contact side was missing but this could quickly be ruled out after taking some basic measurements.

Findings

Without a block diagram of the board, I had to physically feel for which relay was clicking when the starter was comamnded on. I verified that B+ voltage was present on the contact side input, which only left testing for 12 volts on the output. This is when something interesting happened: I had no output at first and wondered if I just didn't have good contact with my probe. So I scratched up the top of the solder joint with my fiberglass pen and remeasured. I still didn't get anything at first, but when I pushed down a little harder with my probe I suddenly had 12 volts present. I repeated the startup procedure and retested at the pin header to find that I now had power here too!

Measurements retaken at relay output and pin header

It seems as though there was a cold solder joint on the output pin of the relay, and just the pressure from pushing on it with my meter probe recreated a connection. I quickly reflowed all the joints on that relay to be thorough, and then decided I wanted to put the circuit under some load to verify the repair. To do this, I used a large 12V work light that draws ~3 amps. 3 amps is considerably less than the pull-in/holding current of the starter solenoid but still enough that it would expose any additional poor connections or issues. I let the controller cycle through multiple crank attempts all the way through before deeming the device as good to go back into service. It will become a shop spare now for our rental fleet in case one fails and a generator goes down.

Incandescent light wired into starter output

Final Thoughts

This was the first time I had a brand new device with a board level issue. Before I got to looking at it, I was concerned it would be some kind of software or firmware level issue I wasn't equipped to properly diagnose. However, I decided not to think that hard into it and just take it as far as I could with what I have. Sure enough, I was able to figure it out rather quickly! A good exercise in keeping things simple, I'm glad I got to get the controller back into working order and reinforce some of the core troubleshooting skills I've developed through my experience thus far.

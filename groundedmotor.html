Grounded motor case study

Using a Fluke Insulation Multimeter to diagnose an electric masonry saw motor that would not start

A 120V brick saw presented with an operator complaint of a 'hum and no start'. They stated that they tried multiple extension cords and power sources to no avail. My first plan for a complaint like this is to perform a basic winding resistance check with my meter, and then perform an insulation (Megger) test to ground to verify the motor is safe to energize.

It is critical to discharge and remove capacitors from single phase motors before performing a Megger test in order to protect myself and prevent the high test voltage from damaging the capacitor. I typically perform the insulation tests at 2x nominal operating voltage as a rule of thumb although this can vary depending on the equipment and its insulation ratings. Although this is a 120V motor, its wiring is rated for 600V; so I tested at 500VDC to start.

Megger Results

The instrument injects a test voltage across the leads and monitors current to determine the resistance. This is similar to normal resistance tests; however, a Megger test occurs at hundreds or even thousands of volts in order to stress test potentially weak insulation in a way that mimics real transient power spikes in application. The Fluke 1587FC I use has a current limit of 1mA, so if the circuit under test has enough leakage to pass more than 1mA, the meter will drop the voltage until current is back below the threshold.

L1 and L2 quickly hit the 1mA limit well before 500V, measuring 0.1 and 0.2 Megohms to ground respectively. Anything less than 10 Megohms is typically too low and a reading less than 1 Megohm is severe degradation. This indicates significant leakage to ground through cord wiring, terminal leads, or the windings. The next step was to test directly at the motor leads inside the terminal box to eliminate the incoming power cord as a potential root cause.

Isolating the Fault

Megger tests of the motor leads at the terminal box resulted in readings of > 550 Megohms to ground, condemning the power cord as the source of the leakage. If I hadn't isolated further, I may have unnecessarily replaced the motor under the assumption that the winding itself was grounded.

Degraded insulation on the cord was a significant finding that likely prevented a future failure; however, it was certainly not causing the initial complaint of humming and not starting.

Further Diagnosis

Failed open capacitor measuring 0uF

I used a power resistor to slowly discharge the start and run capacitors, then verified charge was no longer present with my meter. Capacitance measurement at the start capacitor revealed 0uF, meaning it had failed open and was causing the no-start condition. The run capacitor measured in spec at 40uF but was in poor physical condition. I decided to replace both capacitors and retest.

Retesting

The new capacitors resolved the no-start condition. Once running, I observed a high pitched noise coming from the drive end behind the sheave. Teardown and inspection revealed a severely worn output shaft bearing which would need to be replaced before returning to service.

Verifying the Repair

I inspected the rest of the unit which also prompted replacement of the waterproof switch and both drive belts. With all new parts installed, I verified the saw now starts and runs quietly with no further issues.

Final Thoughts

This case was a cautionary tale in test methodology during diagnostics. No technician or customer would have argued with replacing a motor that 'hums but doesn't start' with a bad Megger reading. In this case, taking the extra time to assess how I was performing my test and what confounding variables might affect the reading got the customer their saw back the next day. If I hadn't proceeded, the saw could have been down for weeks to months waiting for a new motor and it only took me another 5 minutes to perform the definitive test that put me on the right path.
